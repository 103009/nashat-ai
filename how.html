How the AI works

Nashat uses computer vision, the branch of AI that lets computers understand images. It uses a pose-estimation model: a neural network that looks at a photo of a person and predicts where their joints are.

From data to feedback

1 · Data

Labelled photos

Many images of people in different poses. In each one, humans marked where the shoulders, elbows, hips, knees and so on are.

Done by Google
2 · Training

Learning patterns

A neural network guessed the joint positions, compared its guesses with the labels, and adjusted millions of numbers to make the error smaller, over and over.

Done by Google
3 · Prediction

33 body points

On your phone, the trained model gets each video frame and predicts 33 landmarks (x, y, depth) plus how sure it is that each one is visible.

AI, live on your device
4 · Our rules

Angles → coaching

Our code turns the points into joint angles (e.g. hip–knee–ankle). A state machine counts a rep when you go down past a target angle and back up, and checks form.

Written by our team

Why AI suits this problem

No special kit

Motion-capture suits and smart sensors are expensive. Almost everyone already has a phone camera.

Writing rules for pixels is impossible

Nobody could write "if pixel 3,402 is beige, that's a knee". A neural network learns what joints look like from examples, for different bodies, clothes and lighting.

Fast and private

The "lite" model is small enough (about 6 MB) to run many times a second inside a web browser, so video never has to be sent to a server.

What it can do, and what it should NOT do

The AI can

  • Find one person's body points in real time
  • Count reps and spot common form mistakes
  • Give instant feedback when no coach is around

It should NOT

  • Diagnose injuries or replace a doctor or physiotherapist
  • Decide on its own whether it's safe to train. Your body's warning signs always come first
  • Record, store or upload people's bodies or faces

Where it gets things wrong

Hidden joints

If a body part is out of frame or blocked, the model has to guess, and angles can jump around.

Dim light & baggy clothes

Dark rooms, a thobe or abaya, or clothes the same colour as the background make joints harder to find.

Camera angle

A 2D camera sees a flat picture. Push-ups filmed from the front, or a phone tilted down, give wrong angles.
